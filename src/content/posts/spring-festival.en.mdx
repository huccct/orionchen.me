## The New Year Once Had a Particular Smell

Spring Festival is almost here again.

When I was a child, the new year smelled of sulfur left by firecrackers and steam rising from bamboo baskets on the stove. It sounded like adults talking loudly over games of cards in the next room. I did not know what a sense of ritual was. I only knew that the festival meant new clothes, red envelopes, and a holiday when I could play without worrying.

Later, the firecrackers grew quieter and visiting relatives began to feel like an obligation. Questions at the dinner table changed from “Have you grown taller?” to “How is work?” and “Are you seeing anyone?” I used to wait impatiently for Spring Festival. Now the calendar reaches that point, and I pack my things and go home.

It is easy to say that the festival has lost its flavor. But when I think about it, the person experiencing it has changed too.

## Learning to Hear What Is Said at the Table

As a child, I rarely noticed the emotions of adults.

The politeness, comparisons, and brief silences between relatives meant very little to me. If I was happy, I laughed. If I was unhappy, I ran outside to play. I did not have to wonder what else might be hiding behind a sentence.

As I grew older, I learned to recognize those signals. Some questions are simple concern; others also contain judgment. Some of the noise comes from the pleasure of reunion; some of it keeps the room from falling silent.

That does not mean adults are false. Many relationships contain affection, courtesy, competition, and resignation at the same time. As a child, I received only the simplest layer.

The feeling of the new year has not disappeared entirely. It has become mixed with more emotions that I am now able to understand.

## Seeing My Hometown Again

When I was young, that small mountain town was the whole world. Its hills, water, street stalls, and the smell of food drifting from a neighbor's kitchen did not need to be compared with anywhere else.

After seeing more places, I began to notice the narrow streets and limited opportunities when I returned. I saw young people continuing to leave while older residents still sat in the sun outside their doors.

None of this made me dislike my hometown. It simply changed it from the background of childhood into a specific place. It contains familiarity and warmth, but also inconvenience, unfairness, and problems that are difficult to change.

A hometown does not have to become perfect because it is missed. Perhaps real closeness also means acknowledging it as it is.

## Going Back

I no longer expect Spring Festival to feel exactly as it did in childhood.

That freshness belonged to my age then, and to people and surroundings that have since changed. Searching for precisely the same feeling would only confirm that it has gone.

Some things remain. My family prepares food in advance. Familiar people still ask when I will arrive. When I walk into town, I can still recognize its smell in the air.

Spring Festival is no longer a carefree holiday. It is more like a regular return: a chance to see what my hometown has become, and what leaving it has made of me.

For the new year, going home is enough.
