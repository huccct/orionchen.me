## Reappearing from an Ancient Tomb

In 281 CE, during the Western Jin dynasty, tomb robbers opened a Warring States burial site in Ji Commandery. Later accounts say that bamboo slips were burned for light and that many were already broken or incomplete when officials recovered them.

Among the texts later grouped together as the Jizhong manuscripts was a chronicle stretching from high antiquity to the state of Wei during the Warring States period. Because it had been written on bamboo slips, later readers called it the *Bamboo Annals*. Western Jin scholars deciphered, ordered, and edited the slips, bringing a historical work long absent from the transmitted tradition back into view.

The discovery was important, but “excavated” does not mean that the text we read today preserves the tomb manuscript unchanged. The slips were already damaged and disordered, underwent several rounds of reconstruction, and were later lost again. Any reading of the *Bamboo Annals* has to begin with this complicated history of transmission.

## The Texts We Read Today Are Not the Same Book

What is commonly called the *Bamboo Annals* now includes at least two different kinds of material.

| Name | Source | Main problem |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reconstructed Ancient Text | Passages collected from quotations in works such as the *Commentary on the Water Classic* and the *Taiping Yulan* | Reconstructed by later scholars, with much of the original order and context missing |
| Current Text | A more complete version transmitted since the Ming dynasty | Long regarded by many scholars as late or forged, although others argue that it preserves early material |

It is therefore rarely enough to say, “The *Bamboo Annals* records...” We also need to ask which textual tradition the passage belongs to, where it first appears in a surviving quotation, and whether later editors may have rewritten it.

Both the view that the Current Text is largely a forgery and the view that it preserves a substantial amount of early material have appeared in the history of scholarship. Even accepting the second position does not turn every sentence into an untouched Warring States record.

## What Do the Stories of Yao and Shun Prove?

The transmitted *Book of Documents* and *Records of the Grand Historian* portray the transfer of power from Yao to Shun as the selection of a worthy successor and a voluntary abdication. Passages later reconstructed as part of the Ancient Text preserve a very different claim:

> When Yao's virtue declined, he was imprisoned by Shun.

Another quotation says that Shun imprisoned Yao and prevented Danzhu from seeing his father.

These passages appeal directly to our suspicion of perfect sage-kings. But do they prove that Shun seized power in a coup? They do not.

We have no contemporary written record against which to verify events from the supposed age of Yao and Shun. Authors from the Warring States through the Han could all retell remote antiquity through their own political ideas. The abdication story may embody a moral ideal, but the imprisonment story may likewise serve a different theory of power.

The disagreement first shows us that early China possessed more than one memory of antiquity. It does not automatically make the darker account true.

## Two Scripts for Yi Yin and Tai Jia

The *Basic Annals of Yin* in the *Records of the Grand Historian* tells this story: Tai Jia violated established norms, so Yi Yin exiled him to the Tong Palace. After Tai Jia repented, Yi Yin welcomed him back and restored him to the throne.

A reconstructed passage from the Ancient Text gives another version:

> Yi Yin exiled Tai Jia to Tong and then established himself as ruler. Yi Yin took the throne and kept Tai Jia in exile for seven years. Tai Jia secretly escaped from Tong and killed Yi Yin.

One is a story about a virtuous minister correcting his ruler; the other, about a regent seizing power and a former king taking revenge. The latter resembles the political dramas familiar to modern readers and therefore feels especially “real.”

But a sense of realism is not a method for evaluating sources. We can say that Yi Yin held a prominent place in Shang historical memory, but we lack contemporary evidence sufficient to decide between these complete narratives. The more careful approach is to acknowledge competing traditions rather than deliver a verdict on a case more than three thousand years old.

## “Gonghe” Has More Than One Explanation

After King Li of Zhou fled the capital, there followed a fourteen-year period known as Gonghe. The *Basic Annals of Zhou* in the *Records of the Grand Historian* explains it as a joint regency by the Duke of Zhou and the Duke of Shao. A reconstructed passage from the *Annals*, however, says that “Gong Bo He intervened in the royal position,” treating He as a person called Gong Bo He.

Scholars have therefore long disagreed over whether Gonghe referred to joint government by two dukes or a regency by Gong Bo He. The phrase translated here as “intervened in the royal position” might describe interference, temporary rule, or usurpation; four characters alone cannot settle its political nature.

Calling Gong Bo He a successful usurper is as premature as treating the joint regency as an undisputed example of virtuous government.

## Why the Darker Version Feels More Convincing

Moralized history naturally makes us suspicious. When its characters are flawless and every event happens to prove a particular value, we assume that the narrative has been polished.

Yet exposé-style history has its own seduction. It offers the satisfaction of seeing through the story: other people believe in sage-kings, while I know that everything comes down to power. The crueler the account, the closer it seems to human nature.

Historical inquiry cannot choose between those two pleasures. A comforting story is not guaranteed to be true, but neither is a dark one.

The value of the *Bamboo Annals* lies in forcing us to see these traditions together. It prevents the *Book of Documents* and the *Records of the Grand Historian* from remaining the only versions and draws attention to how different texts shaped the political images of Yao and Shun, Yi Yin, and Gong Bo He.

## How to Read Conflicting Sources

When I encounter material like this now, I try to ask several questions:

1. Does the passage come from a complete transmitted text, or from a quotation reconstructed by later scholars?
2. How far is the surviving text from the event it describes?
3. Is there contemporary writing, archaeological evidence, or another independent source that supports it?
4. What political or moral interpretation does each version serve?
5. Am I lowering my standard of evidence because one version fits my worldview better?

These questions do not produce an immediate answer. They do help prevent “another account” from becoming “the truth that was covered up.”

## Closing Thoughts

I was first drawn to the *Bamboo Annals* because it tore open an ancient world that had been made too orderly. After checking the evidence again, I still value the book, but no longer treat it as a collection of revelations.

It is better understood as a reminder. The history we read was shaped by records, editing, loss, and reconstruction across different periods. Every version, including the one that best matches modern intuition, deserves the same scrutiny.

Historical literacy is not the habit of rejecting the orthodox account. It is knowing how far the evidence allows us to go when texts disagree.

The *Bamboo Annals* does not decide whether Yao abdicated or Yi Yin usurped the throne. What it preserves is the possibility of asking those questions again.
